Reading: A Fresh Start

Read the article quickly and choose the best title from the box. There is one extra title you do not need.

When Maria Kowalczyk handed in her notice at the bank where she had worked for eleven years, her colleagues thought she had lost her mind. She had a steady income, a generous pension scheme and a manager who trusted her completely. "Everyone kept asking me why I would give all that up," she says, laughing. "The honest answer was that I felt stuck. Every morning I woke up dreading the commute, and every evening I came home exhausted but strangely unfulfilled."

Maria had always been passionate about gardening. For years she had spent her weekends looking after an allotment on the outskirts of town, and her friends often turned to her for advice about plants that were wilting or seeds that refused to sprout. Slowly, an idea began to take shape. What if she could turn her hobby into a small business?

At first, she was reluctant to take the risk. She drew up a detailed business plan, sought advice from a local enterprise agency and attended an evening course in bookkeeping. "I didn't want to jump in at the deep end," she explains. "I wanted to make sure the numbers added up before I committed to anything." She also set aside enough savings to cover her living costs for a whole year, just in case things went wrong.

Eighteen months later, her company designs and maintains small urban gardens for busy professionals who have balconies or tiny backyards but no time to look after them. She now employs three part-time staff and has a waiting list of clients. That does not mean everything has been plain sailing. "There were weeks when I barely broke even," she admits. "In the first winter, almost nobody wanted a garden, and I seriously considered going back to banking. But I gritted my teeth and used those quiet months to build a website and get in touch with property developers."

According to career coach Daniel Hughes, Maria's story is becoming increasingly common. "People no longer expect to stay in the same job for life," he says. "Many of my clients are in their thirties or forties, and they are looking for work that feels meaningful rather than simply secure. The key is to be realistic. You need a safety net, you need to be willing to learn new skills, and you need to accept that setbacks are part of the process."

Hughes also points out that change is not always voluntary. Companies restructure, departments are merged and roles become redundant. "When change is forced on you, the emotional reaction can be quite intense," he says. "People go through something similar to grief: denial, anger, and eventually acceptance. The ones who bounce back fastest are usually those who have a strong support network and who focus on what they can control instead of what they can't."

For Maria, the biggest lesson has been about resilience. "I used to think confident people never doubted themselves," she says. "Now I know that they simply carry on despite their doubts. If you wait until you feel completely ready, you will probably wait forever."

Listening: A New Flatmate

You are going to hear a conversation between two friends, Tom and Ellie. Tom has just moved into a shared flat. Listen and answer the questions.

Ellie: So, how's the new place? You moved in last weekend, didn't you?
Tom: Yeah, on Saturday. It's been a bit of a rollercoaster, to be honest.
Ellie: Really? What happened?
Tom: Well, the flat itself is great. It's bright, spacious, and it's only a ten-minute walk from the station. But my flatmate, Jake, is going to take some getting used to.
Ellie: Oh no. Is he messy?
Tom: Not exactly messy. He's just got very different habits. He goes to bed at about four in the morning and plays the guitar until then. Quietly, he says, but the walls are paper-thin.
Ellie: That sounds exhausting. Have you talked to him about it?
Tom: I brought it up on Tuesday. I tried to be tactful. I said something like, "Look, I don't want to make a fuss, but I start work at eight." He was actually really apologetic. He said nobody had ever complained before.
Ellie: Well, that's a good sign. At least he didn't get defensive.
Tom: True. We agreed that he'll stop playing at midnight on weeknights. Let's see if he sticks to it.
Ellie: And what about the bills? Sharing costs can be a nightmare.
Tom: We've set up a shared account for rent, electricity and internet. We each pay in a fixed amount at the beginning of the month. It's pretty straightforward.
Ellie: Sensible. And cleaning?
Tom: That's the next conversation. At the moment I seem to be the only one who knows where the vacuum cleaner is.
Ellie: You'd better sort that out early, before it turns into a real source of resentment.
Tom: I know, I know. I'm planning to suggest a rota. Fingers crossed he agrees.
Ellie: I'm sure he will. It sounds like he's reasonable, just a bit of a night owl.
Tom: Exactly. Anyway, the best thing is the neighbourhood. There's a fantastic bakery on the corner, a park where I can go running, and a little cinema that shows old films on Sundays.
Ellie: That sounds lovely. I'm a bit jealous, actually. My street is all traffic and takeaways.
Tom: You should come round on Sunday. We can grab some pastries and catch a film.
Ellie: Deal. As long as Jake isn't giving a concert.

Answer the questions.

1. How long has Tom been living in the flat?
2. What are the advantages of the flat?
3. What problem does Tom have with Jake?
4. How did Jake react when Tom complained?

Grammar: Used to, be used to, get used to

Look at the examples from the unit and answer the questions.

a) I used to think confident people never doubted themselves.
b) My flatmate is going to take some getting used to.
c) Maria is used to working long hours now.
d) It took Tom a few days to get used to the noise.

Which sentence describes:
1. a past habit or state that is no longer true?
2. something that is familiar and no longer strange?
3. the process of becoming familiar with something?

Remember: after "be used to" and "get used to" we use a noun or the -ing form of the verb. After "used to" for past habits we use the infinitive without "to".

Writing: An email of advice

Your English friend Sam has written to you. Read the extract from the email and write a reply of 140–190 words.

"I've finally decided to move to Poland for a year to work as an English teacher! I'm really excited, but also a bit scared. I don't speak any Polish, I don't know anyone there, and I've never lived abroad before. What should I do before I go? And how can I settle in quickly once I arrive? Any tips would be amazing."

In your email, you should:
- react to Sam's news
- give advice on how to prepare before leaving
- suggest ways to meet people and settle in
- offer to help in some way

Model answer

Hi Sam,

What fantastic news! I'm thrilled you've decided to take the plunge, and I'm sure you'll have an unforgettable year.

Before you leave, I'd recommend learning a few basic phrases in Polish. Even simple greetings will make a huge difference, and people really appreciate the effort. It might also be worth sorting out your bank account and health insurance in advance, so you don't have to deal with paperwork as soon as you arrive.

Once you're here, try to say yes to every invitation, even if you feel tired or shy. Your colleagues at the school will probably be your first friends, so don't hesitate to ask them for recommendations. You could also join a sports club or a language exchange — they're great ways to meet locals who want to practise their English.

Finally, remember that it's completely normal to feel homesick at first. Give yourself time to adjust.

If you need somewhere to stay for the first few nights, you're more than welcome to crash on my sofa!

Take care,
Kasia
